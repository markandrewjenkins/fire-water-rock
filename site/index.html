What are the odds of a big earthquake?

Gutenberg–Richter recurrence from 55 years of USGS records: small quakes are common, big ones rare, in a straight line on a log scale. Extending that line gives the yearly rate of larger events.

Magnitude–frequency

Earthquakes per year at or above each magnitude (log scale), with the fitted line.

The one this can't see

This catalogue covers crustal and deep (intraslab) earthquakes. It can't capture the Cascadia subduction zone, which hasn't ruptured since January 1700, before records began. Geologists estimate a magnitude 9 roughly every 500 years on average, from offshore turbidite and coastal subsidence records. That risk sits on top of the odds shown here.

    Method

    Wildfire starts. I count new fires per day per fire centre and cause (lightning vs people) for April–October, 2000–2025. Weather features are the centre's ECCC station heat, three-day heat, temperature anomaly, rain over 1, 7 and 30 days, days since rain, and a dryness index (heat accumulated since the last soaking). The model is Poisson gradient boosting, validated leave-one-season-out. The forecast runs it on Environment Canada GEM forecasts via Open-Meteo.

    Rivers. Each live reading is ranked against that gauge's daily means within ±7 days of the date, 1991 onward (flow where it's measured, otherwise water level).

    Floods. I fit a Gumbel distribution by maximum likelihood to water-year (Oct–Sep) annual maximum daily flows before the 2021 event, with a 400-draw bootstrap for the range. A 3-parameter GEV is kept as a sensitivity check. For the Tulameen and Chilliwack it puts an upper bound on flows below what 2021 delivered, which is itself a sign of how far outside experience the storm was.

    Earthquakes. Magnitude of completeness is estimated by maximum curvature + 0.2. The b-value is the Aki–Utsu maximum-likelihood estimate with a bootstrap range, and probabilities assume a Poisson process.

    Honest limitations

    • Lightning itself isn't in the data. The model sees only heat and dryness, so it can say a day is primed for lightning fires but not where storms will cross.
    • People-caused fires depend on campfire bans, holidays and enforcement as much as weather. The model barely beats climatology at ranking whole seasons for them.
    • One weather station stands in for each fire centre, and some centres are the size of a small country.
    • Daily mean flow understates instantaneous peaks: the Coquihalla's real crest was higher than shown. Extrapolating any fit beyond about twice the record length is shaky, so read 500-year figures as "far outside experience".
    • Earthquake odds extrapolate a 55-year catalogue to magnitudes it barely contains.
